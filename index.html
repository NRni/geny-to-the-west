What this guide is for

Empower, don’t prescribe

Gene editing has left the lab bench and entered headlines, clinics, and ordinary arguments. Geny to the West is a short companion for readers who want the ideas straight, and who would rather leave with better questions than with someone else’s conclusion.

What we do

  • Help you question a claim yourself
  • Show you how to read a CRISPR headline, because those facts move
  • Say when the science is solid, and when it isn’t

What we don’t do

  • Hand you a fact sheet and call it done
  • Tell you which side is right
  • Scare you, rank people, or sell this as a designer-baby story

Not simply inform. Not advocate. Empower.

You leave with questions, not a verdict.

The line this guide keeps returning to: Ask better questions. Decide for yourself.

Who it’s for

Youth & Future Generations

Target audience

Teenagers, those who will come into contact with and use gene editing technology in the future

Objective

First, To fill the knowledge gap among young people.

For example: Some consumer genetic advertisements imply that by undergoing genetic testing, one can predict a child’s future intelligence quotient and athletic talent. Many self-media also portray CRISPR as the “magic scissors of life” that can arbitrarily modify human traits. Our manual aims to fill this middle ground, using simple and accurate content to enable people to have the ability to judge and understand basic knowledge.

Second, Provide a set of reusable tools for critical questioning.

For example: When young people see an advertisement like “Genetic testing helps you select babies with high intelligence”, they no longer get directly attracted but will actively ask questions: Can this technology really work now? What are the off-target risks? Who is selling this service and profiting from it? Will it bring about new social discrimination?

Planned result

  1. Be able to distinguish between facts, science fiction imagination, and commercial marketing hype
  2. Master the framework for critical questioning and no longer passively receive information
  3. Be able to recognize the fallacies of genetic determinism
  4. Be able to understand key ethical risk concepts

Stakeholders / investors

Scientists and educators

Scientists need their work explained without the hype, so trust survives the headline. Teachers need a way to teach a topic that isn’t settled, without telling students who won. The path is scientists, this guide, educators, then you.

Foundation

Replicators, then the rules

Start with the selfish gene. A gene wins by getting copied.

01

Replicator vs vehicle

The foundation

Replicators (genes). The information trying to get copied.

Vehicles (organisms). The bodies genes build to carry and protect them. Genes don’t “want” anything. They just shape tendencies. A “selfish” gene simply means whatever gets copied most.

02

The replicator triad

The rules of success

To win the game of evolution, a gene needs three things.

Fecundity. To copy fast and often.

Fidelity. To copy accurately.

Longevity. To persist and last a long time.

Chapter

Gene editing technology

Sub-chapter

CRISPR

CRISPR-Cas9 finds a 20-letter address and cuts there. The cell does the rewriting.

01

The setup

Finding the target

CRISPR-Cas9 is a biological find-and-cut system. It has two parts.

The guide RNA (gRNA). A 20-letter sequence that acts as a GPS address. It scans the DNA looking for an exact match.

The Cas9 protein. The molecular scissors that perform the cut.

The guide RNA locates the exact 20-letter match in the genome, binds to it, and Cas9 snips the DNA at that location.

02

The edit

The cell’s repair

CRISPR creates a double-strand break. The cell’s own repair machinery does the rewriting. The outcome depends on how the cell repairs the break.

NHEJ (Non-Homologous End Joining), the glue job. The cell rushes to glue the broken DNA ends back together. It is fast, and messy. It often accidentally adds or deletes a few letters, an indel. That scrambles the gene’s instructions and turns the gene off, a knockout. Scientists use it to destroy a bad gene or disable a viral gene.

HDR (Homology-Directed Repair), the rewrite. The cell uses a matching DNA sequence as a template to rebuild the broken strand. Scientists inject a custom DNA template alongside the CRISPR tools. The cell reads that template and copies it into the gene, a knock-in. Scientists use it to correct a disease-causing mutation or insert a new function.

HDR is highly accurate and very inefficient. The cell prefers the fast glue job, so scientists have to work hard to push the cell into the rewrite.

03

The scope

Somatic vs germline

Where the edit is applied decides how long the genetic change lasts. That is longevity.

Somatic edits. Body cells, such as blood or liver. The change fixes the patient and stops with them.

Germline edits. Embryos, eggs, or sperm. The change is copied into every cell of the future person.

A somatic edit has medium longevity: one lifetime. A germline edit has infinite longevity: it is passed down to every generation after that person.

04

The imperfection

Off-target effects

The guide RNA is 20 letters long. Sometimes another part of the genome has 19 of those 20 letters. Cas9 might mistakenly cut there as well, and that cut is an unintended mutation.

Evolution requires accurate copying. That is fidelity. An off-target effect is a failure of fidelity, a loss of accuracy in the edit.
